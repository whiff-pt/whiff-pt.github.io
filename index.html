        What these terms mean
        Wind
        Direction the wind is blowing from, and its speed in mph. A south wind pushes the mill plume north. Light winds are usually worse than strong ones: a breeze delivers the smell undiluted, a gale shreds it.
        Off-axis
        How far you are, in degrees, from the centre of the plume's path. 0° means the plume is aimed straight at you; 90° means it's passing to one side; 180° means it's heading the opposite way.
        Downwind alignment
        Off-axis converted to a 0–100 score, widened to account for how much Port Townsend's winds wander hour to hour and how uncertain any forecast's wind direction is.
        Stability (the one everybody asks about)
        How strongly the air is layered, which decides whether the smell rises away or stays at nose level. On a clear, calm night the ground cools, cold air settles underneath warmer air above — a temperature inversion — and it acts like a lid. High stability means that lid is on and the plume is trapped near the ground. Low stability means the air is churning and carries the smell upward. This is why the mill can be unnoticeable at noon and overwhelming at 3am with no change at the mill itself.
        Decoupling
        When the wind is very light and that lid is very low, wind direction stops meaning anything at all — the air near the ground detaches from the flow above and drifts wherever the terrain sends it. On those nights the smell can reach you from a direction the forecast says is impossible.
        Fog & humidity
        Damp air holds odor compounds at nose level, and fog means a shallow, saturated, very stable layer. Both make things worse.
        Cold-air pooling
        Heavy odor gases and cold air both flow downhill and collect in low spots, so low-lying and shoreline addresses catch more.
        Rain washout
        Rain scrubs sulfur compounds out of the air. Shown so that higher is worse, like the other bars — 100 means dry, low means it's raining and clearing.

      Which model should I use?
      
          Best available is the right choice unless you have a reason
        otherwise. Open-Meteo
        picks whichever forecast is most skilful for this location and stitches them
        together, giving five nights of outlook.
          
      
          HRRR is the US weather service's
        High-Resolution Rapid Refresh
        — a 3 km grid updated hourly. Its fine grid resolves the Quimper Peninsula's
        terrain better than the others, which matters for exactly the light-wind
        drainage nights this app cares about. The trade-off is that it only runs
        48 hours ahead, so you lose the longer outlook.
          
      
          ECMWF is the European model, generally the best in the world
        at large-scale pattern forecasting but on a coarser grid here.
          
      
          If tonight matters and the three disagree, that disagreement is itself the
        useful signal: it means the forecast is genuinely uncertain.
